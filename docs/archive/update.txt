Now, the subscription tiers — I want to walk you through these properly

The pricing you have designed is good and well thought out. But I want to be straight with you about what it involves to build, because from the outside it reads like a pricing page, and underneath it is a considerably larger piece of engineering than the rest of JurisMon put together. I would rather explain that now than discover it halfway.

Here is the distinction that matters.

Taking the money is the small part. Enforcing what the money buys is the large part.

Think of it as the difference between a ticket booth and a stadium. Selling three kinds of ticket at the booth is straightforward. Making sure the person holding a Starter ticket cannot sit in the Business section, cannot bring four friends, and cannot come back twenty times — that is turnstiles, a seating map, and staff. The booth is a day of work. The rest is the stadium.

Let me take your tiers one line at a time.

"1 user seat / up to 5 seats / unlimited seats"

Right now JurisMon has no user accounts at all. There is one admin login for you, and the search page is open to anyone who has the link. There is no sign-up, no customer login, no concept of an account that a subscription belongs to.

So "seats" is not a setting to switch on. It means building: customer registration, email verification, login and password reset, the idea of an organisation that owns a subscription, inviting teammates into it, removing them, and counting them against a limit. That is a user management system, and it has to exist before the word "seat" means anything.

"500 / 5,000 / 25,000 queries per month"

For this, every single search has to be attributed to an account, counted, stored, and reset on that account's own billing date — not on the first of the month, because a customer who subscribes on the 14th has a limit that runs to the 14th.

Then there is what happens at the limit. Do they get blocked outright? Warned at 80 percent? Shown an upgrade prompt? Do failed queries count? Each of those is a decision, and each one has to be built and tested, because a metering bug either gives service away or wrongly cuts off a paying customer.

"API access: 1,000 / 75,000 programmatic requests per month"

This one is effectively a second product. The current API exists for our own web page to talk to our own backend. A public API that customers build against is a different thing: it needs API keys that can be issued, shown once, rotated and revoked, per-key request counting, rate limiting so one customer cannot slow the service for everyone, proper error responses, and documentation so developers know how to use it. Customers who build against an API also expect it not to change underneath them.

"Monthly and annually with 20% discount"

This part is genuinely quick — it is six plans instead of three, and I can create all six. The complication is what happens afterwards: a customer on Starter monthly who upgrades to Pro annually mid-cycle has to be credited for the unused part, moved to the new plan, and have their quotas adjusted. Plan changes are where subscription systems usually go wrong.

"Custom data exports" and "priority / dedicated support"

Exports are a real feature — choosing what to export, in what format, and generating it without the server stalling on a large request. Support tiers are mostly process on your side, but if it is promised on the pricing page, the system has to know who is entitled to what.
